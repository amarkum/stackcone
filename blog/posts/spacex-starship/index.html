SpaceX Starship and the Falcon Drawdown

September 2026 · Published by Amar Kumar

Falcon 9 is still the workhorse of Earth orbit. SpaceX is already planning to fly it less.

President Gwynne Shotwell told Time the company would do about 140–145 Falcon launches this year, down from 165 Falcon 9 flights last year. That is not a retreat from space. It is a bet that Starship takes Starlink, lunar landers, and eventually orbital compute — and that Falcon tails off as that vehicle becomes routine.

This is a status report for operators: where Starship actually is after Flight 13, why Falcon cadence is being managed down, and what the launch gap means if you depend on a ride to orbit.

The Falcon 9 drawdown

Falcon’s recent history is a straight ramp: 96 Falcon flights in 2023, 134 Falcon 9 + Heavy in 2024, 165 Falcon 9 in 2025. Four-day Cape turnarounds became normal, then rare again.

Planned Falcon flights this year sit below last year’s peak on purpose.

  • Vandenberg — most Starlink Falcon 9s, still as tight as every three or four days
  • Cape Canaveral — about one Falcon 9 per week, closer to the 2023 rhythm
  • Starbase — Starship test and early operational flights

Shotwell’s line: fly a lot this year, then tail off Falcon as Starship comes online. Internal Starlink still eats most Falcon manifests. Merchant customers get whatever is left.

Starship after Flight 13

Starship is a two-stage methane/LOX stack: Super Heavy plus the Ship, both meant to be reused. Wikipedia’s running tally through 24 July 2026 lists 13 flights, 8 successes, 5 failures. 2025 was messy. The V3 / Block 3 vehicle then posted two successful flights this year.

Flight 13 (24 July 2026) changed the tone: first Starlink V3 batch on a Starship, an in-space engine relight, and the gentlest Ship ocean splashdown SpaceX has shown. Super Heavy is the more mature catch candidate. The Ship tower catch is the next advertised leap.

Elon Musk said Flight 14 would try to catch the Ship with the tower arms unless data review found problems. That implies an orbital-class profile Starship has not fully closed: around the Earth and back to Starbase.

Starlink V3

Starlink is why Falcon 9’s cadence exists. It is also why Starship has to work. V3 satellites are larger. Putting them on Falcon forever is the wrong factory shape. Moving that traffic to Starship is how SpaceX frees Falcon — or parks it — as Starship pads multiply in Texas and Florida.

Orbital data-center nodes launched on Starship are still a slide until the vehicle is orbital, catchable, and flying often. This year’s job is proving reuse, not announcing constellations.

Artemis gaps

NASA selected Starship as a human landing system. Two problems still sit in front of a crewed landing:

  1. Ship catch and rapid reuse
  2. Orbital propellant transfer — not yet demonstrated

Until those close, Artemis dates slip with the vehicle. Treat Starship news as test-program progress, not an operational lunar ferry.

Who else can fly

The medium-lift gap is the interesting market. Falcon Heavy and New Glenn fight the high end. Rocket Lab’s Neutron, Stoke’s reusable designs, and Chinese heavy lift are the “not Starship, not Falcon 9” options. None replace SpaceX volume yet. A Falcon drawdown before Starship is operational is a sector-wide capacity story.

FAQ

Is SpaceX flying fewer rockets?

Fewer Falcons than last year, by design. Starship flights are still counted in the teens.

Did Starship reach routine operations?

No. Flight 13 was a strong test. Routine means catch, refurbish, and fly again on a schedule.

Why does Starlink V3 need Starship?

The satellites are larger. Starship is the vehicle sized for the next constellation.

What is still missing for Artemis?

A reliable orbital Ship, tower catch / reuse, and demonstrated propellant transfer.
