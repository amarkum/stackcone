# SpaceX Starship and the Falcon Drawdown

**September 2026 · Published by Amar Kumar**

Falcon 9 is still the workhorse of Earth orbit. SpaceX is already planning to fly it less.

President Gwynne Shotwell told Time the company would do **about 140–145 Falcon launches in 2026**, down from **165 Falcon 9 flights in 2025**. The slack is not a retreat from space. It is a bet that **Starship** takes Starlink, lunar landers, and eventually orbital compute — and that Falcon tails off as that vehicle becomes routine.

This post is a status report for builders and operators: where Starship actually is after Flight 13, why Falcon cadence is being managed down, and what the 2026 launch gap means if you depend on rides to orbit.

## The Falcon 9 drawdown

Falcon 9's recent history is a straight ramp: **96 Falcon flights in 2023**, **134 Falcon 9 + Heavy in 2024**, **165 Falcon 9 in 2025**. Four-day pad turnarounds at the Cape became normal, then rare again.

In 2026 SpaceX is splitting the work:

- **Vandenberg** — most Starlink Falcon 9s, still as tight as every three or four days
- **Cape Canaveral** — about one Falcon 9 per week, closer to the 2023 rhythm
- **Starbase** — Starship test and early operational flights

Shotwell's line was blunt: fly a lot this year, then **tail off Falcon as Starship comes online**. That is a factory and pad decision, not a demand collapse. Internal Starlink still eats most Falcon manifests. Merchant customers get whatever is left.

## Where Starship stands after Flight 13

Starship is a two-stage methane/LOX stack: Super Heavy plus the Ship, both meant to be reused. Wikipedia's running tally through **24 July 2026** lists **13 flights, 8 successes, 5 failures**. KeepTrack's 2024–2026 cut is similar in spirit: a messy 2025, then **two successful 2026 flights** on the **V3 / Block 3** vehicle.

**Flight 13 (24 July 2026)** is the one that changed the tone:

- First batch of **next-generation Starlink V3** satellites on a Starship
- In-space engine relight
- The gentlest Ship ocean splashdown SpaceX has shown
- Super Heavy still the more mature catch candidate; the **Ship tower catch** is the next advertised leap

Elon Musk said after the flight that, unless data review found problems, Flight 14 would **try to catch the Ship with the tower arms**. That attempt implies a true orbital-class profile Starship has not fully closed: around the Earth and back to Starbase, not another suborbital splash.

V3 is also the variant SpaceX has pointed at for **NASA Artemis** lunar landing work — stronger Raptor 3 engines, the configuration they intend to operationalize.

## Starlink V3 and why cadence matters

Starlink is why Falcon 9's cadence exists. It is also why Starship has to work.

V3 satellites are larger. Putting them on Falcon forever is the wrong factory shape. Moving that traffic to Starship is how SpaceX frees Falcon for national-security and commercial slots — or parks Falcon entirely as Starship pads multiply in Texas and Florida.

SpaceX has also talked about **orbital data-center nodes** launched on Starship (with xAI in the public narrative). That is still a slide until the vehicle is orbital, catchable, and flying often. The 2026 job is proving reuse, not announcing constellations.

## Artemis and the milestones still open

NASA selected Starship as a human landing system for Artemis. Two engineering problems still sit in front of a crewed landing:

1. **Ship catch and rapid reuse** — Flight 14 is the public next test.
2. **Orbital propellant transfer** — required for a lunar lander stack; not yet demonstrated.

Until those close, Artemis dates slip with the vehicle, not the other way around. Treat 2026 Starship news as **test-program progress**, not as an operational lunar ferry.

## The 2026 launch gap

Global orbital attempts in 2026 are pacing **slightly below 2025**. Three SpaceX-shaped reasons:

| Factor | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| Falcon 9 drawdown | Fewer of the world's most reliable flights |
| Starship not yet cadence | Test flights do not replace 100+ Falcon Starlink missions |
| Competitors slow | New Glenn, Neutron, and others have not absorbed the leftover manifests |

Pad scarcity is real. Starbase and Florida are crowded. Vandenberg added a Blue Origin New Glenn pad selection. Stoke and Relativity are building at the Cape. Rocket Lab, Northrop, and Firefly remain in the Wallops conversation.

If you need a ride in 2026–27, assume **Falcon is tighter than 2025**, Starship is not a catalog item yet, and backup providers still have their own delays.

## Who else can fly

The medium-lift gap is the interesting market. Falcon Heavy and New Glenn fight the high end. Rocket Lab's **Neutron**, Stoke's fully reusable designs, and Chinese heavy lift (Long March 10-class) are the "not Starship, not Falcon 9" options.

None of them yet replace SpaceX volume. That is why a Falcon drawdown *before* Starship is operational is a **sector-wide capacity story**, not just a SpaceX story.

## FAQ

### Is SpaceX flying fewer rockets in 2026?

Fewer Falcons than 2025, by design. Starship flights are still counted in the teens, not the hundreds.

### Did Starship reach routine operations?

No. Flight 13 was a strong test. Routine means catch, refurbish, and fly again on a schedule. That is still ahead.

### Why does Starlink V3 need Starship?

The satellites are larger. Falcon can loft them inefficiently. Starship is the vehicle sized for the constellation SpaceX wants next.

### What is still missing for Artemis?

A reliable orbital Ship, tower catch / reuse, and demonstrated propellant transfer. Landing hardware is not the only long pole.

### Should operators plan on Starship cargo in 2026?

Plan on Falcon if you can get a slot. Treat Starship as a 2026 test campaign that *might* open early operational windows — not as a booked replacement for Falcon 9.
